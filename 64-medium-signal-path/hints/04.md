# Complexity

**O(n) time.** One stack-driven walk to fix the order, one sweep over that order,
`O(1)` work per desk in each: two comparisons against zero, two additions, one
`max`, one write. Every desk is pushed once, popped once, and settled once, and
nothing is read twice.

**O(n) space, on purpose.** The order list holds `n` desk numbers and `down` holds
`n` totals. The traversal stack is bounded by the height of the hierarchy, which is
`n` on a line, so it is `O(n)` too. Dropping the order list in favour of a genuine
single-pass post-order does not move the bound — the stack alone reaches `n` on
the shapes this challenge ships — and the input is `O(n)` regardless.

**The recursive version's space is the trap, not its time.** Written recursively
the auxiliary space is `O(h)`, the height: about `log2(500000) = 19` frames on an
even split, and free. On a line `h = n`, and 500000 frames is a `RecursionError`
in Python whatever `setrecursionlimit` says, and a blown 8 MB stack in Rust or C at
a few dozen bytes a frame. Both seeded recipes are deep by construction — one is a
line, the other a 250000-desk spine — so `O(h)` is `O(n)` on every large case here.
That is why `03.md` insists on the explicit stack: same space, different failure.

## Why the obvious alternative loses

**Walking each arm from scratch** is the literal reading of hint 1, and it is what
`rotten/main.py` does: take every desk in turn as the highest desk, and for each of
its arms walk that arm's whole branch, carrying the running total downward, to find
the best descending chain in it. Correct for every input the README allows. Its
cost is the sum of all branch sizes, and that sum has a name: it is the sum of
every desk's depth.

Which is why the shape matters more than `n` here:

- on an **even split** the average depth is about `log2(n)`, so the naive version
  costs about `n log n` — about `9 * 10^6` at `n = 500000`, well inside any
  budget. A balanced large case would not catch it at all.
- on a **line** the average depth is `n/2`, so it costs `n(n-1)/2` —
  `1.25 * 10^11`.

So the shape that breaks the naive version is the *deep* one, which is the same
shape that breaks a recursive implementation of the intended one. Both seeded
recipes are deep for exactly that reason and neither is balanced. Depth is the one
axis this challenge varies, and it is doing two jobs at once.

**Memoising the arm walk** is worth recognising for what it is: cache `down[c]` the
first time an arm is walked and the sum of branch sizes collapses from
`sum of depths` to `n`. That is not a repair of the naive version, it *is* the
algorithm in `03.md` with the recursion still in place — so it clears the time wall
and walks straight into the depth one.

**Anything that keeps the pairs.** Precomputing depths and prefix totals from the
root, then answering a pair `(a, b)` in `O(1)` from its deepest shared desk — the
`60-medium-venue-ancestor` machinery — is correct and each pair really is constant
time. There are `1.25 * 10^11` of them. Hint 1's reduction exists to delete the
pairs, and no per-pair cleverness substitutes for it.

## The wall

`rotten/main.py` re-walks every branch from scratch: `sum of depths` steps against
the intended `n`, which on these two recipes is `1.25 * 10^11` and `6.25 * 10^10`
steps against `5 * 10^5` settled desks.

| recipe | shape | n | naive steps | answer | golden | naive, native |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `09_large_skewed_chain` | one 500000-desk line, sides alternating, numbers shuffled | 500000 | 124999750000 | 608344100298 | ~0.8 s | still running at 246 s |
| `10_large_caterpillar_desks` | 250000-desk spine with a leaf on every spine desk, numbers shuffled | 500000 | 62500000000 | 276359507560 | ~0.9 s | 1062 s |

Measured with a temporary optimized native control on the two seeded inputs. The
control is a literal transcription of `rotten/main.py` — same outward walk, 64-bit
totals, desk numbers as `int`, an explicit stack instead of recursion — compiled
`cc -O3 -march=native -funroll-loops`, which is the fastest thing anyone would
plausibly hand-write while still re-walking each branch. On
`10_large_caterpillar_desks` it ran to completion in **1062 s**, taking exactly the
`62500000000` steps the shape predicts and printing the same answer as
`golden/main.py`. On `09_large_skewed_chain` it was still going when stopped at a
hard **246 s** cap, having settled 32768 of 500000 desks and 8.2 * 10⁹ of its
1.25 * 10¹¹ steps. Both sustain only 0.033–0.059 * 10⁹ steps per second despite the
trivial inner loop: the shuffled numbering means every step chases a pointer into an
8 MB working set, so the walk is memory-latency-bound rather than
instruction-bound, and that is inherent to re-walking branches rather than
something a compiler flag recovers.

Against the 5 s Rust/Go/C solver budget that is a margin of **212x** on the recipe
that finished and at least **49x** on the one that did not, while `golden/main.py`
finishes in *Python* in under a second on both — so essentially none of the gap is
parsing. `rotten/main.py` itself sustains about 3.5 * 10⁶ steps per second, which
puts the two full scans near 9.9 and 5.0 hours. `make bench` does not wait: the
runner kills the process group at the timeout.

**The two recipes fail differently on purpose.** `09_large_skewed_chain` has no
turns anywhere — every desk has one sub-desk, so `meets[v]` never combines two arms
and the whole answer comes out of the value propagated upward. That makes it the
depth recipe: 500000 frames for anything recursive, a 500000-entry stack for
anything iterative, and, since the contributions are a random walk across the full
`±10^9` range, a winning run that is a window somewhere in the middle rather than
the whole line, which is the clamp doing its job. `10_large_caterpillar_desks` is
the opposite: every one of its 250000 spine desks is a genuine turn with a leaf on
one side and the rest of the firm on the other, and about half the leaves lost
money, so the clamp fires at about half the turns. Note what the winning chain
there can and cannot be: it runs down a stretch of spine and may attach one leaf at
each of its two ends, never one leaf per spine desk, because leaving the spine is
the one turn it gets. A solver that clears one recipe and not the other has built
half of hint 2.
