# Hint 1

> Spoilers. Open only when stuck, and only one file at a time.

**No strategy has a value of its own.** In Example 3 strategy `1` earns `20` and
needs the `-30` prerequisite `0`, so on its own it is worth `-10` and looks like a
reject. Together with strategy `2` it is worth taking, because the `-30` is paid
once and shared. That is not an oddity of that example, it is the whole problem:
the price of a strategy depends on which *other* strategies were chosen, so there
is no order to sort the candidates in and no per-strategy test to apply. The thing
being chosen is the set, all at once.

So stop looking for a rule that decides one strategy and look at the shape of the
set instead. A choice partitions the `n` strategies into two groups, activated and
not, and the prerequisite list constrains that partition in exactly one way: no
pair `[a, b]` may have `a` in the activated group and `b` outside it. The other
crossing — `b` activated, `a` not — is always fine.

**Now change what you are measuring.** Add up every positive contribution and call
it the **ceiling**: what the firm would book if prerequisites were free. Any valid
set `S` falls short of the ceiling by an amount you can read off the partition,
strategy by strategy:

```
total(S) = ceiling
           - (every earner left out of S,   each costing its own profit)
           - (every loss-maker put into S,  each costing its own loss)
```

The ceiling is a constant, so *maximising the total is minimising that shortfall* —
and every term of the shortfall is a price charged to one strategy for landing on
one particular side of the partition. Nothing in it depends on how many other
strategies came along.

You now have: two groups; a per-strategy price for being on the wrong side; and a
list of ordered pairs, one crossing of which is forbidden outright. Three parts, no
sequencing, no per-item decision.

That is a familiar object with a familiar name. What is the cheapest way to split a
graph in two called, and what computes it?
