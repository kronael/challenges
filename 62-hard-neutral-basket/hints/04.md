# Complexity

**O(2^(n/2) · n) time.** Building the two halves is `O(2^(n/2))` additions.
Sorting one half of `2^(n/2)` nets is `O(2^(n/2) · log 2^(n/2))` =
`O(2^(n/2) · n/2)`. The sweep is `2^(n/2)` binary searches of `n/2` steps each,
another `O(2^(n/2) · n/2)`. At `n = 38` that is roughly 524288 sorted keys and
524288 searches — about ten million operations, on an input of 38 numbers.

**O(2^(n/2)) space.** Two arrays of `2^(n/2)` 64-bit nets, 4 MB each at
`n = 38`. Nothing else scales with the input. (Schroeppel–Shamir gets the space
down to `O(2^(n/4))` at the same time bound, using four quarters and a heap; it
is not needed at these limits.)

## Why the obvious alternatives lose

**Enumerating every basket** is the direct reading of the statement and it is
what `rotten/main.py` does: run a mask over `0 .. 2^n - 1`, sum the positions the
mask selects, keep the closest net. Correct for every input the README allows, and
`O(2^n · n)`, because nothing is shared between one basket and the next.

**A table indexed by reachable net** — the pseudo-polynomial move that solves
`17-medium-knapsack` in `O(n · capacity)` — needs a cell per reachable net. Nets
here span `-3.8 * 10^13 .. 3.8 * 10^13`, so that is `7.6 * 10^13` cells; even one
bit per cell is 9 TB. This is exactly why `|exposures[i]|` is `10^12` and not
`10^4`: the bounded-total DP is not slow here, it is impossible, and the halving
is what replaces it. A hash set of reachable nets has the same problem from the
other direction — up to `2^38` distinct nets to store, and each of the seeded
cases has all 38 exposures distinct, so almost nothing collides.

**Greedy** in any form. "Walk the positions largest first and take one whenever
it moves the net closer to the mandate" fails on `{6, 5, 5}` with a mandate of
10: it takes the 6, then a 5 to reach 11, then refuses the last 5 because 16 is
worse, and reports 1. The answer is 0 — the two 5s, without the 6. There is no
exchange argument to rescue it, and no ordering that fixes it: closest-subset-sum
is NP-hard. Halving does not make it polynomial, it makes the exponent `n/2`.

**Branch and bound** with a suffix-magnitude bound (`prune when the remaining
positions cannot possibly close the current gap`) is a real technique and it
crushes lopsided books, where a few huge positions dominate. It is not what this
challenge measures, and it is not a bound you can promise: the seeded cases have
38 exposures of about the same magnitude as the mandate, which is where that
pruning is weakest, and its worst case is still `2^n`. Meet in the middle costs
`2^(n/2)` on every input, lucky or not.

**Assembling the halves eagerly.** Building `front`, then adding every `f` to
every `b` to get one big list of nets, sorts `2^n` numbers and is worse than the
mask scan. The pairing must stay implicit: 524288 questions asked of a sorted
list, never 275 billion answers materialised.

## The wall

`rotten/main.py` sums all `2^n` baskets from scratch: `O(2^n · n)` against the
intended `O(2^(n/2) · n)` — 275 billion baskets against 524288 sorted nets and as
many searches.

| recipe | shape | n | answer | golden | naive, native |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `09_large_signed_spread` | 38 positions drawn across the full `±10^12` range, mandate drawn independently | 38 | 6 | ~1.0 s | ~370 s |
| `10_large_same_sign_split` | 38 same-sign even positions, odd mandate at half the desk's total, so no basket can land on it | 38 | 7 | ~1.0 s | ~370 s |

Measured with an optimized native (`cc -O2 -march=native`) control on the two
seeded inputs. The literal transcription of `rotten/main.py` runs 88.6 million
baskets per second, which projects the full `2^38` scan to about 3100 s. Replacing
it with the fastest brute force anyone would plausibly hand-write — Gray-code
enumeration, one add and one compare per basket, no re-summing — gets to 745
million baskets per second and still projects to about 369 s. Both were still
running when killed at a hard 5 s cap, and again at a hard 30 s cap. Against the
5 s Rust/Go/C solver budget that is a margin of roughly 74x for the fast native
brute force and 620x for the naive one, and `golden/main.py` does the same work in
*Python* in under a second, so essentially none of the gap is parsing: the inputs
are 553 and 540 bytes.

The two recipes fail differently. `09_large_signed_spread` mixes signs, so nets
crowd the mandate from both directions and the sign of a position says nothing
about whether it belongs in the basket. `10_large_same_sign_split` is one-signed
with the mandate at half the total — the balanced-partition instance, where the
answer is provably non-zero (every exposure is even, the mandate is odd) so no
solver gets to stop early and no lucky exact hit passes for a proof.
