# Hint 3 — why the obvious alternatives are wrong here

**Scanning forward from each day** is the direct reading of the statement, and
it is what `rotten/main.py` does: for day `i`, walk `i+1, i+2, …` until a lower
settlement appears. It is correct and it is O(n²). The shape that kills it is
not exotic — a series that mostly rises is the normal case, and there every walk
runs to the end of the series without finding anything.

**Walking right to left and jumping over resolved days** is the tempting repair:
if day `j` is already answered, hop `answer[j]` days instead of stepping one at
a time. It is genuinely faster on friendly data, but nothing bounds how many
hops a single day makes, so it buys no worst-case guarantee — and the hop must
be special-cased for a day whose answer is `0` (never undercut), which is easy
to get subtly wrong with equal prices. The stack gives the linear bound outright.

**A range-minimum structure** (sparse table, or a segment-tree descent for "the
leftmost index right of `i` whose price is below `x`") answers the question, in
O(n log n) time with O(n log n) or O(n) memory and a descent routine to debug.
It also answers far more than was asked — any range, any threshold — when the
queries here arrive in day order and each one is `i+1 … n-1`.

**It is not the fixed-window deque from `13-medium-sliding-window-max`.** There,
an index leaves the structure for one of two reasons: it was beaten, *or* it
aged out of the window of `k` days, so the structure never holds more than `k`
entries and every position reports an aggregate over a bounded span. Here there
is no `k` and nothing ages out. An index leaves only when it is undercut, the
structure can hold all `n` days at once — a strictly rising series never pops
anything — and the value reported is per day, not per window. Carrying the
window bookkeeping over produces wrong answers; drop it.
