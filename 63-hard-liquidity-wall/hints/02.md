# Hint 2

**Level `i`'s run stops at the first level, on each side, that rests less than
it.** That is the whole content of "the widest run in which `i` is the
shallowest": walk down until a level rests less than `quantities[i]`, walk up
until a level rests less than `quantities[i]`, and everything strictly between
those two blockers is the run that `i` limits.

So each level needs two indices:

```
left[i]   = the nearest index below i whose quantity is smaller   (or -1)
right[i]  = the nearest index above i whose quantity is smaller   (or n)
candidate = quantities[i] * (right[i] - left[i] - 1)
```

The `- 1` is there because both blockers sit *outside* the run; drop it and every
candidate is one level too wide, which fixtures `01` through `08` all catch.
The two fallbacks, `-1` and `n`, are best not written as special cases either:
treat index `-1` and index `n` as levels resting less than anything the book can
rest, and then "the nearest shallower level" always exists and one formula covers
a run that reaches the top or the bottom of the book. Fixture `05` rises the
whole way and fixture `06` falls the whole way, so in each of them every winning
run is pinned against one end; fixture `04` is a book of one level, which needs
both fallbacks at once.

**You have computed one of these two boundaries before.**
`59-medium-price-undercut` asks for the first later day that settles strictly
below a given day. That is `right[i]` — and that challenge wanted only the
*distance* to it, in one direction, one answer per day. Here you need the
boundary *index*, on *both* sides, and what you build from the pair is a product,
not a distance. A long shallow shelf can out-lift a single deep level or lose to
it, and neither boundary on its own tells you which: fixture `16` is a book where
the shelf wins, fixture `15` a book where the one deep level wins.

**Equal quantities are where this gets delicate.** "Smaller" has to mean
*strictly* smaller on at least one of the two sides. Take a plateau of three
levels resting 500 each and make both sides non-strict — "the nearest index whose
quantity is smaller *or equal*" — and each of the three finds its neighbour as a
blocker, so each reports a run of one level and the answer comes out 500 instead
of 1500. Strict on both sides is correct, and gives every level of a plateau the
full plateau width. Strict on one side and non-strict on the other is *also*
correct, which is worth understanding rather than avoiding: the levels in the
middle of a plateau then under-report their run, but the one at the non-strict
end still measures the whole plateau, and the answer is a maximum, so nothing is
lost. Non-strict on both sides is the one combination that breaks, and fixtures
`02`, `14` and `18` all fail loudly on it.

Which leaves the only remaining question. Finding either boundary by *walking* is
what the statement invites, and it is quadratic — a book whose quantities only
rise makes every upward walk run to the far end. What would make the two boundary
scans cost a constant each, amortized, across the whole book?
